Learn the science: 

Read the Davis formula: Dc = 1.4 × G × N^(−0.48) — this is the equation that determines which particles get sorted. G = gap between pillars, N = array period. This is your core physics.
Understand two modes: zigzag mode (small particles follow flow) vs bump mode (large particles deflect laterally). Your simulation models both.
Research context: DLD is used RIGHT NOW to isolate circulating tumor cells from blood for cancer early detection. That's your "why it matters" story for judges.
Watch: search YouTube for "deterministic lateral displacement microfluidics" — there are great visualizations of real chips working.

Python simulation: 

Step 1 — particle trajectory sim: Use NumPy to model particles of different diameters moving through a pillar array. Apply the Davis formula to calculate critical diameter. Plot which particles zigzag vs bump using matplotlib.
Step 2 — geometry sweep: Your original research question — vary pillar gap (G) and array period (N) across 10–20 configurations and compute sorting efficiency for each. Which geometry best separates "cell A" (8µm) from "cell B" (12µm)?
Step 3 — flow rate analysis: Model how changing flow velocity affects sorting. Too fast = particles don't sort correctly. Find the optimal flow rate range for your best geometry.
Step 4 — web dashboard: Build a Flask + HTML/CSS/JS frontend where you input pillar gap, period, and particle size and it shows predicted trajectory in real time. Original software contribution — no one else will have this.

Libraries: NumPy, matplotlib, Flask, maybe SciPy for fluid equations. All free, all in Python which you already know.

CAD: 

Design 2–3 pillar array geometries in SolidWorks — cylindrical pillars are easiest to start. Design the mold as a raised positive (pillars stick up) so elastomer casts around them.
Scale up by 10–20x from real chip dimensions (real chips have 10–50µm features; your printer does ~200µm minimum). That's fine — you're demonstrating the geometry and principle, not building a clinical device.
Add inlet and outlet ports (3–4mm diameter cylinders) for tubing connections. Add a flat base at least 3mm thick so the mold doesn't warp during printing.
Print at 0.1mm layer height for smoothest pillar walls. 

Physical prototype: 


Cast SYLCAP elastomer into your 3D printed mold. Cure in oven. Peel, punch ports, bond to microscope slide.
Connect silicone tubing to ports. Use your syringes to manually flow two differently-colored food dye solutions — one "small particle" stream, one "large particle" stream.
Image with your LCD microscope. Does the flow split as your simulation predicted? Even partial agreement is a valid scientific result worth reporting.
Later: connect your Arduino stepper motors to control flow rate precisely and match the flow rates you simulated in Python.
